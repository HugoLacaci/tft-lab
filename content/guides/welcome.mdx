This page assumes nothing. You do not need to know League of Legends or to have watched anyone play. Each section ends with a pointer to the short lesson that drills it on a real board.

## What is TFT?

Teamfight Tactics is a game where you build a small team of champions and that team fights for you. You never control a champion directly. You decide who is on the team, what they carry and where they stand; then the fight plays out on its own and you watch.

Eight players start every game, each with their own board, their own gold and their own health (HP). A game is a series of rounds. In most rounds you are paired against one of the other seven, the two teams fight, and whoever loses the fight loses some HP. Nobody loses champions in a fight: your team is back at full strength next round. The only thing that goes down is your HP, and it never comes back on its own.

When your HP reaches zero you are out, and your placement is the position you went out in. The last player alive is 1st.

One more thing before anything else: all eight players buy champions from the same shared pool. There is a fixed number of copies of each champion in the game (30 of every cheap one, fewer of the expensive ones), and every copy someone else buys is one you cannot. This shapes the whole game.

Placement is what you are playing for. In ranked play 1st to 4th gain rating and 5th to 8th lose it, so a 4th is a good result, not a failure. The first lesson drills what a round costs you, what the shared pool means for the champions you want, and why a top-4 finish is the goal.

## The shop and star-ups

At the start of each round your shop shows five champions, each with a price from 1 to 5 gold; the expensive ones are stronger and rarer. Buying puts the champion on your bench, the row of nine slots below the board, until you drag it onto the board.

If you do not like the five on offer, you can refresh the shop for 2 gold and see five new ones. This is the first trap in the game: refreshing feels free because 2 gold is small, but you get a new shop for nothing at the start of every round, and 2 gold spent now is 2 gold not earning interest later. Refresh when you are looking for something specific, not to see what turns up.

The most important thing the shop does is let you upgrade champions. Own three copies of the same champion and they merge automatically into one 2-star version, with much more health and damage than a single copy. Nine copies make a 3-star, stronger again, but nine is a long road and not something to chase by accident. Selling a champion gives you its price back (a 2-star of a 1-cost sells for the 3 gold you spent), so a copy you later sell costs you nothing but bench space.

The shop question every round: is there a copy of a champion I already own? If so, buy it. A 2-star 1-cost beats most single-copy 3-costs in the early game, and it cost you 3 gold. The shop lesson drills exactly that: spotting the third copy, remembering that refreshing costs 2, and selling what you no longer need.

## Traits and breakpoints

Every champion has two or three traits, shown under its picture. One is usually a class (what it does: a tank, an archer, a spellcaster) and the others an origin (where it comes from). Traits are how champions work together.

A trait switches on when enough champions with that trait are on the board at once. The numbers where it switches on are its breakpoints. A trait with breakpoints 2, 4 and 6 gives a bonus at two champions, a bigger one at four and a bigger one still at six. The panel beside your board lists your traits with a count like "Defender 1/2": one Defender, two needed for the first bonus. One of two is nothing yet. Each breakpoint is all or nothing.

The bonuses are large. A defensive trait at its first breakpoint might give your whole team armour; an attack trait might give everyone attack speed and its own champions more. A board with two or three active traits beats the same number of champions chosen at random.

This gives you a rule for the shop: when two champions cost the same and neither is a copy of something you own, take the one that reaches a breakpoint with the champions already on your board. That is how a team composition (a comp) forms without you memorising anything. The traits lesson drills reading the panel, choosing the unit that activates a trait, and recognising when a trait at 1 of 2 is not helping you.

## Items

Items make a champion much stronger, and they come in halves. A half is called a component: a sword, a bow, a rod, a vest, a belt and so on. Components drop from the rounds against monsters and from the carousel (both explained below). Drag two components onto the same champion and they combine into a completed item, which is worth far more than the two halves apart. Any two components combine; which two you pair decides what you get.

A champion holds up to three items, so give some thought to who gets them, but do not freeze. One rule covers almost every case: damage items go on the champion that deals your damage, usually a ranged attacker or spellcaster at the back; defensive items go on the champion that gets hit first, your tank at the front. A sword on a tank is wasted, and armour on an archer is wasted.

The most common beginner mistake is not a wrong pairing but no pairing at all: components sitting on the bench for rounds while the player waits for the perfect combination. A component on the bench adds nothing to any fight. A completed item on the board, even a slightly imperfect one, wins fights now, and winning now protects your HP. The items lesson drills combining components, choosing the holder and getting items off the bench.

## Gold and interest

Gold arrives at the start of every round. From round 2-2 on you get 5 gold a round as base income (the first few rounds pay a little less while the game ramps up). On top of that come two bonuses.

<InterestTable />

The first is interest: 1 extra gold for every 10 you are holding when the round ends, up to 5 extra at 50. A player sitting on 50 gold earns 10 a round; a player at 20 earns 7; a player at 0 earns 5. The 50-gold player gets twice the income of the 0-gold player for doing nothing, every round, and the gap compounds over a game. This is why experienced players sit on a pile of gold: the pile is paying them.

The second is streak gold. Winning several fights in a row pays extra: 1 gold at two or three in a row, 2 at four or five, 3 at six or more. Losing several in a row pays exactly the same. A streak in either direction is worth money; a broken run (win, loss, win, loss) is worth nothing. It is not a reason to lose on purpose; it is a reason not to panic when you lose a few in a row while saving.

The rounding matters. Holding 38 gold and seeing a 2-gold champion you only slightly want? Buying drops you to 36 and costs no interest; you are still at three 10s. Holding 41? The same buy drops you to 39 and costs 1 gold a round until you climb back over 40. The gold lesson drills this: why 50 earns more than 20, what a streak pays, and whether your last few gold are better spent or held to reach the next 10.

## Levels and XP

Your level is the number of champions you can put on the board. Level 4 means four champions, level 7 means seven. You gain 2 XP for free every round, and you can buy 4 XP for 4 gold as often as you like. When the bar fills, you level up and get one more slot.

A level does two things. The obvious one is a bigger team: one more champion in every fight. The less obvious one is the shop. Expensive champions only appear once you reach higher levels, and the odds shift as you go.

<OddsTable />

At level 4 the shop is more than half 1-costs. At level 8, 4-costs are almost a third of what you see. Levelling is how you move from cheap units to strong ones; at level 5 the shop will almost never show you a 4-cost.

There is a standard schedule most of the lobby follows: level 4 by the start of stage 2, 5 in the middle of stage 2, 6 early in stage 3, 7 at the start of stage 4, 8 in stage 4 or early 5. Following it keeps you the same size as the players you fight. The levels lesson drills when one more unit is worth 4 gold, and what the shop shows at each level.

## The flow of a game

The game is divided into stages, and each stage into rounds. The clock reads "3-2": stage 3, round 2. Stage 1 is a short warm-up, a carousel and a few rounds against monsters that give you your first champions and items. From stage 2 on, every stage has seven rounds with the same shape:

- Rounds 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6 are fights against another player. Lose one and you lose HP.
- Round 4 is the carousel: all eight players walk around a ring of champions, each carrying an item, and pick one. Players with less HP pick first.
- Round 7 is against monsters (a PvE round: player versus environment). Beat them and they drop components or gold; no player can take HP from you here.

Three times per game, at 2-1, 3-2 and 4-2, you choose an augment: a permanent bonus for the rest of the game, picked from three options. Some give gold, some combat strength, some a trait. You cannot change your mind later, so read all three before clicking.

Two more facts. The HP you lose for a defeat grows with the stage: a few HP in stage 2, a lot in stage 5, which is why the early game is for building and the late game is for being strong. And every fight is preceded by a planning timer of around thirty seconds; that is when you buy, sell, level and move champions. The fight itself you just watch. The flow lesson drills reading the clock, knowing which round comes next and remembering the augment rounds.

## The board

Your board is four rows of seven hexes. The row nearest the middle of the screen, where the enemy comes from, is the front; the row furthest away is the back. Melee champions have to walk to their target, so the enemy's fighters head for whichever of your champions is closest and hit that one.

That gives you the whole of beginner positioning in two lines. Champions that can take hits (tanks) stand in the front row, so they are what the enemy reaches first. Champions that deal damage from a distance (archers, spellcasters) stand in the back row, where they keep attacking for a long time before anyone gets to them.

Keep the frontline together in the middle. If your tanks stand at opposite corners, an enemy walking up the middle strolls between them and reaches your back row. Side by side, the enemy bumps into them and stays there.

Then watch your fights. If an enemy champion jumped on your archer and killed it in two seconds, move the archer next round: to the other side, or behind a second body. For now: front and back, together in the middle, one change when something goes wrong. The board lesson drills placing a tank and a carry, keeping the front together and moving a unit after a loss.

## Your first game plan

You do not need to know which champions are strong this patch to place well. A plan built from the ideas above beats most players in the lower ranks, because most of them do not follow one.

Stage 2: buy every copy of a champion you own; put your 2-stars on the board; combine your first two components on whichever ranged champion you have; level to 4 and then 5 on schedule. Do not refresh the shop. You will lose some fights, and the HP is cheap here.

Stage 3: level to 6 at 3-2, keep buying copies and keep saving; the goal is 50 gold by the end of the stage. Pick a direction now: look at the traits you already have two or three of and the items you already made, and decide which champions you are collecting. From here every buy should be a copy of one of them or a unit that reaches one of their breakpoints.

Stage 4: level to 7 at 4-1 and then spend. Refresh the shop, now with a purpose, until the champions you are collecting are 2-star and your traits hit their breakpoints, and stop with a few gold left rather than at zero. This is what the saving was for. Put every item on the board. Then keep the board as strong as it can be every round, because losses now cost real HP.
